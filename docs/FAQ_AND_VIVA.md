# FAQ and viva preparation

Questions an examiner, reviewer or user is likely to ask, with short honest answers. Numbers come from
[MODEL_AND_EVALUATION.md](MODEL_AND_EVALUATION.md).

## The problem and the approach

**What exactly does the system detect?**
Whether a whole photo-like image was produced by a text-to-image generator (Stable Cascade, FLUX, Midjourney, Imagen,
Kandinsky and similar) or captured by a camera. It does not detect face swaps in videos, localised edits (inpainting,
Photoshop) or text generated by AI.

**Why not reuse the original face model?**
It was trained only on face crops from the teammate datasets. AI images today are mostly full scenes from modern
generators, which it was never shown. The face models remain in `models/` for comparison and rollback.

**Why tiles at native resolution instead of resizing the image?**
Generators leave traces in fine pixel statistics (noise, upsampling patterns). Resizing to a fixed input size blurs or
destroys them. On the same data and validation split, the native-tile model scored a val composite of 0.923 against
0.870 for the resized variant. Tiling also handles any image size with one model.

**Why EfficientNet-B0?**
Good ImageNet features with only 4 M parameters: it trains on a 6 GB laptop GPU (about 6 minutes per epoch on 47 k tiles)
and runs on a CPU in well under a second per image. Larger backbones were not tried because of time and GPU limits - so
we cannot claim B0 is the best choice, only that it was feasible and worked.

**What is the training data?**
47,716 tiles: AI images from many Community Forensics generators plus a pool derived from the Community Forensics
evaluation set (with its benchmark families removed), and real photos from LAION, Open Images, ImageNet, COCO, FFHQ, LHQ
and other sources. Format bias was reduced by re-compressing PNG images to JPEG with qualities drawn from the real photos.

## Evaluation

**How do you know the model was not tuned on the test set?**
The benchmark families (Midjourney, FLUX, Imagen, Stable Cascade, Kandinsky 2.2) were fixed by a hash rule in code before
any image was viewed, and no image of these families was used for training, calibration or selection. The epoch, the
Grad-CAM layer and the demo images were chosen on `val`; temperature and band on `calib`. The benchmark was scored once.
An earlier benchmark (CompEval-v1) *was* looked at before the decision to retrain, so it was retired and replaced.

**Is CompEval-v2 a standard benchmark?**
No. It is **project-constructed**: a stratified sample this project drew from the public Community Forensics evaluation
set. Results are not directly comparable with published leaderboards.

**Why report ROC-AUC rather than accuracy?**
AUC measures how well the score *ranks* AI images above real ones, independent of any threshold and of the class
balance (the benchmark is 61 % AI). Accuracy depends on both. We report both: AUC 0.909, accuracy 78.8 %, balanced
accuracy 80.7 % at the 0.5 threshold.

**What do the confidence intervals mean?**
95 % bootstrap intervals: the benchmark images were resampled 300 times (separately within each class) and the metric
recomputed. AUC 0.900-0.918 means another sample of the same sources would likely give an AUC in that range. It says
nothing about generators outside the benchmark.

**Why is AUC higher after JPEG compression (0.954) than on the original images (0.909)?**
Every AI image in the benchmark is a PNG file, while most real photos are JPEG. The model was trained to resist the
"PNG means AI" shortcut and seems to read PNG slightly *against* the AI class. Re-compressing everything removes the
format difference. That is why the original-image figure is the headline: it is the conservative one.

**What are the weak spots?**
Imagen 3 (50 % detected), Midjourney v6.1 (53 %) and v5.2 (56 %); ImageNet-style real photos (21 % flagged as AI);
low-resolution images (quarter size: AUC 0.763, a third of real photos flagged).

**The model gets two demo images wrong. Why show them?**
Because a demo of only correct answers would misrepresent a 72.5 % / 88.8 % detector. The gallery shows a correct AI
image, correct real photos, two inconclusive cases, one false alarm and one miss.

## Calibration and the verdict

**What does "calibrated" mean here?**
A temperature T = 1.618, fitted on a separate calibration split, rescales the network's output so that its probabilities
match observed frequencies on that split. It changes confidence, not ranking (AUC is unchanged).

**Top-label ECE is 0.022 but class-wise ECE is 0.136. Which is right?**
Both, measuring different things.
* *Top-label ECE* compares the confidence in the predicted class with how often that prediction is right. At 0.022, the
  confidence of the decided class is trustworthy.
* *Class-wise ECE* compares P(AI) itself with the actual AI frequency in each score bin. On these unseen generators P(AI)
  runs too low in the middle: images scored 30-40 % were AI 59 % of the time. The mean P(AI) is 0.48 against a 61 % AI
  share. The likely cause is that these generator families were never seen in training.

The app shows both numbers and warns that mid-range scores understate AI.

**What is the inconclusive band and how was it chosen?**
If the calibrated P(AI) is between 19 % and 73 %, the app answers *inconclusive*. The edges were set on the `calib` split
so that each decided side reached about 90 % class-balanced precision. On the benchmark: 45.4 % of images are
inconclusive, and decided answers are right 93.3 % of the time.

**Isn't abstaining on 45 % of images useless?**
It is a trade-off we made deliberately. Without the band every image gets an answer and 21 % of answers are wrong; with
it, fewer than 7 % of decided answers are wrong. For a tool whose mistakes can hurt people, saying "I don't know" is
better than a confident guess. The raw probability is still shown.

## Explanations

**What does the heat map show?**
Grad-CAM at layer `blocks.4`: which regions of each 256 px tile increased the score for the chosen answer, stitched back
onto the image. Areas no tile covered are dimmed.

**Does a hot region mean that part was generated or edited?**
No. The whole image is either generated or not; the map shows what *the model* relied on, which can be ordinary texture
or edges.

**How do you know the map is meaningful at all?**
Deletion/insertion test: removing the highlighted regions lowered the model's score faster than removing random regions
(and revealing them restored it faster) in 76 % of 80 correctly classified validation tiles. A weight-randomisation
sanity check gave maps uncorrelated with the real ones (Spearman ρ ≈ 0.01). Layer `blocks.4` was chosen because it did
best on this test (`bn2`: 58 %, `blocks.2`: 46 %).

## The training history

**Why was the shipped model taken from epoch 4 of a crashed run?**
Two runs accidentally wrote to the same checkpoint and log paths. The 8-epoch run's best checkpoint (val composite 0.866
at epoch 6) was overwritten, and its values survive only in console output. The second run crashed in epoch 5 with a
Windows paging-file error; its epoch-4 checkpoint (0.850, recorded in the log and meta file) is what we calibrated,
benchmarked and ship. A clean retrain was not done before the demo; it might score slightly higher. We report this
rather than claim the 0.866 number.

**`general_native_a` scored 0.923 on validation but only 0.688 AUC on a benchmark. What happened?**
Its validation set was too similar to its training data. On real web photos (LAION) and modern commercial generators it
failed: only 38 % of LAION photos were kept real. That is why the second model added LAION/ImageNet real photos and
commercial-generator images, and why its validation set is larger and harder. The jump to 0.909 used a different
benchmark version, so it is indicative, not a controlled comparison.

## Limits, ethics and engineering

**Can the detector be fooled?**
Yes. Downscaling, heavy blur, screenshots and adversarial perturbations are known attacks on detectors of this kind;
only downscaling, blur and compression were measured here. New generators will likely be detected less well.

**Should it be used to accuse someone of faking an image?**
No. It is a research prototype with a measured false-alarm rate (11 % of real photos at the 0.5 threshold, 3 % with
the band). The UI says so on every result.

**Privacy?**
Images are processed locally, in memory, on a server that listens only on `localhost`. Nothing is uploaded or stored.

**Licensing?**
Training and benchmark data are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, so the trained model should be treated as non-commercial research
material. The code has no licence file yet. See [LICENSES.md](LICENSES.md).

**Known security issues?**
`torch 2.6.0` has published advisories (most in features this app does not use, such as `torch.compile`, JIT and
specific operators; one affects loading untrusted checkpoints, while this app loads only its own, checksummed weights).
Pillow was upgraded to 12.3.0 to clear its advisories, and uploads are decoded only as JPEG/PNG/WEBP/BMP/TIFF. See
[SBOM.md](SBOM.md).

**What would you do next?**
1. A clean, complete retrain with separate output paths, and more Imagen / Midjourney training data.
2. A benchmark with matched file formats (AI images saved as JPEG as well as PNG).
3. Per-generator calibration or a stronger backbone, measured on the same held-out families.
4. Upgrade PyTorch to a version without the open advisories and re-verify all numbers.
